## Target share

Target share is the percentage of a team's total pass attempts that were
directed at one player over a given window. A receiver with a 25% target share
was the intended receiver on one in four of his team's throws.

It measures opportunity rather than production, which is why it is more stable
week to week than receiving yards and is usually a better guide to what a
player will do next.

## Snap share

Snap share is the percentage of a team's offensive snaps a player was on the
field for. A player can hold a high snap share while producing very little, and
that gap is usually the most useful signal in the data: the coaching staff is
choosing to play him.

Snap share is reported separately for offence, defence and special teams.

## Air yards

Air yards are the distance a pass travels in the air, measured from the line of
scrimmage to the point where the receiver is targeted. They are credited on any
target, whether or not the pass is caught.

Air yards separate a receiver's role from his results. Two receivers with equal
yardage can have very different air-yard totals, and the one accumulating more
air yards is being used further downfield.

## Yards after catch

Yards after catch, or YAC, is the distance a receiver gains after securing the
ball. Yards before catch, or YBC, is the complement. Splitting receiving yards
into the two describes how a player earns production: a high-YAC receiver is
creating after the catch, a high-YBC receiver is winning downfield.

## Average depth of target

Average depth of target, or aDOT, is a player's mean air yards per target. A
low aDOT indicates a short, high-volume role near the line of scrimmage. A high
aDOT indicates a downfield role with fewer, more variable opportunities.

## WOPR

WOPR stands for Weighted Opportunity Rating. It combines target share and air
yards share into a single measure of how central a receiver is to his offence.
The conventional formula weights target share at 1.5 and air yards share at
0.7.

It exists because the two inputs capture different things: a player can lead a
team in targets while another leads it in air yards, and WOPR ranks them on
combined opportunity.

## Opportunity share and touch share

For running backs, opportunity share is the percentage of a team's combined
carries and targets that went to one player. Touch share counts only carries
and receptions actually recorded.

Opportunity share is generally preferred because a target that falls incomplete
still represents a decision by the offence to involve that player.

## Red zone share

Red zone share is the percentage of a team's opportunities inside the opponent's
20-yard line that went to one player. Because touchdowns are worth six points
and cluster in the red zone, a small difference in red zone share produces a
large difference in fantasy scoring.

## Why usage is reported separately from production

Production tells you what happened. Usage tells you what role a player was
given. A player whose production fell while his usage held steady is a
different case from one whose usage collapsed, even though a single week's
fantasy points look the same.
