## Expected points added

Expected points added, or EPA, measures how much a single play changed a team's
expected scoring outcome. Every game situation — down, distance, field position,
time remaining — carries an expected point value. EPA is the difference between
the expected points after a play and before it.

A four-yard gain on third and two is a positive EPA play. A four-yard gain on
third and ten is a negative one. This is why EPA is preferred to raw yardage
for evaluating efficiency: it understands situation, and yardage does not.

EPA per play is the usual rate form, for both offences and defences. For a
defence, negative EPA allowed is good.

## Success rate

Success rate is the percentage of plays with positive EPA. It answers a
different question from EPA per play: whether an offence is consistently
staying on schedule, rather than whether it produces occasional large gains.

An offence can have strong EPA per play and weak success rate, which describes
a boom-or-bust unit dependent on explosive plays.

## Completion percentage over expected

Completion percentage over expected, or CPOE, compares a quarterback's actual
completion rate to the rate expected given the difficulty of his throws —
depth, pressure, receiver separation, and similar factors.

A quarterback who completes 68% of passes on a schedule of deep throws may have
a higher CPOE than one who completes 72% on short passes. It isolates accuracy
from the offence's design.

## Pressure rate and sack rate

Pressure rate is the percentage of dropbacks on which the quarterback was
hurried, hit, or sacked. Sack rate counts only completed sacks.

Pressure rate is the more stable of the two, because whether a pressure becomes
a sack depends heavily on the quarterback.

## Explosive plays

An explosive play is conventionally a run of 10 or more yards or a pass of 15
or more yards. Definitions vary between sources, so the threshold should be
stated whenever the term is used.

## Time to throw

Time to throw is the average seconds between snap and pass release. It
contextualises pressure rate: a quarterback holding the ball longer will face
more pressure regardless of how his line performs.

## Yards per route run

Yards per route run divides receiving yards by routes run rather than by
targets or games. It is regarded as the best single efficiency measure for
receivers, because it accounts for the opportunities a player was on the field
for but was not targeted on.

## A caution on cross-source comparison

Advanced statistics are computed differently by different providers. Two
sources can report different EPA for the same play because they use different
expected-points models. Numbers should not be mixed between sources without
saying so.
